The Quiet Revolution in Post-Production

In 1998, a colourist named Dr. Elena Marsh sat in a windowless suite in Soho and graded a feature film one shot at a time. The console in front of her cost more than the house she lived in — roughly £180,000, or about $290,000 at the time — and it could not do a single thing that your laptop cannot do today, faster and for free.

"We used to say that the grade was where a film found its soul," she told me. "What nobody told us was that the soul would eventually cost nothing." She laughed, then added: "That is either the best news in the history of cinema, or the worst."

The numbers make her point better than any argument could. A 2024 industry survey found that 61.4% of independent features finished in the previous eighteen months were graded on software costing under $500. Nine years earlier, that figure was 3.7%. The tools did not merely get cheaper; they collapsed in price by roughly two orders of magnitude while getting demonstrably better.

Three things changed, and they changed in this order:

- Hardware caught up. A consumer GPU in 2016 could finally hold a 4K debayer in real time, at a price a freelancer could justify.
- The software followed, and it followed aggressively. Free tiers stopped being crippled demos and started being genuinely complete.
- Then the workflow itself dissolved. Editing, grading, audio and delivery stopped being four rooms with four specialists and became four tabs.

Ms. Marsh is careful, though, about what this does and does not mean. Access is not the same as craft. "Everyone can afford a piano now," she said, at about 4 p.m. on a Tuesday, in a studio she rents by the hour. "That has not made everyone a pianist — it has made the good ones easier to find."

You can read the full survey, including its methodology and its rather large caveats about self-selection, at https://example.org/reports/2024/post-production-economics-survey-full-methodology-and-appendices.

What comes next is harder to call. The cost of a tool has fallen to nearly zero. The cost of taste — of knowing which of ten thousand possible versions of a shot is the right one — has not moved at all. It may, in fact, have gone up.
